What is mobile data usage?

Mobile data usage is how much internet you're burning through when you're not connected to Wi-Fi. It's the cellular data your phone pulls from network towers instead of your home router. Every scroll through Instagram, GPS search for the nearest cafe, or video call home eats into your mobile data allowance. For travelers, it’s pretty important since you're often relying on cellular data networks instead of Wi-Fi connections.

Frequently asked questions

  • Data throttling happens when your internet speed is intentionally slowed by your carrier or ISP. It often kicks in after you’ve used up your high-speed data allowance, but it can also occur during peak hours as part of network management. The result is slower web browsing, longer photo load times, and choppy video calls. Throttling is usually defined in your plan’s fair usage policy (FUP) — it doesn’t matter if you’re using a regular SIM or an eSIM. You can avoid the slowdown by picking a plan with more high-speed data or one that doesn’t cap your speed after a limit.
  • How much mobile data you need depends on your usage habits — light users who only check emails and maps might need 1-2 GB for a week, while heavy streamers and photo uploaders may need 10 GB or more. Use our data usage calculator to get a personalized estimate based on how much time you spend on social media, streaming, navigation, and video calls. This helps you choose the right plan size without overpaying or running out of data mid-trip.
  • The average person uses 5-15 GB of mobile data monthly, but it varies widely. Usage also depends on your device settings and your daily habits — streaming in HD uses three times more data than standard quality, and automatic photo backups can drain data without you noticing.
  • Basic activities like web browsing, social media scrolling, and sending messages use less data. You can perform these activities for dozens of hours per GB. For video streaming, 1 GB is usually enough for about 2-4 hours in standard definition. In HD, the usage varies — at 720p it’s roughly 1 GB per hour, while 1080p can range from 1.5 to 3 GB per hour depending on the platform.
    Music streaming is much kinder to your data usage — you can get around 20-25 hours from 1 GB. Data-heavy activities eat through your allowance fast — video calls use about 150-300 MB per hour, online gaming typically consumes 40-150 MB per hour (depending on the game), and downloading large files like apps or movies can use several hundred MB in minutes.
  • Video streaming is the absolute worst offender — HD video can burn through 3 GB per hour, while standard definition still chomps through about 1 GB hourly. Music streaming is at around 150 MB per hour for high-quality tunes. Social media scrolling eats up roughly 100-200 MB per hour, depending on how many videos decide to auto-play while you're mindlessly browsing. But the real data destroyers? Automatic app updates, cloud photo backups, and downloading large files — these functions can demolish gigabytes in minutes.
  • In most cases, browsing the web uses less data than scrolling through social media apps. Simple websites with mostly text and a few images are pretty gentle on your data. Data usage for Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook is often high because these apps are packed with auto-play videos, high-resolution photos, and frequent updates.
  • Streaming movies or YouTube in HD is a classic data-usage culprit. Background apps can quietly munch through your data when you’re not looking, too. Big downloads like games, updates, or cloud backups also add up quickly. And if you’re sharing eSIM data via hotspot and don’t have it locked down, someone else might be hitching a ride on your network!
    To find the reasons for increased data usage, check your phone’s data usage reports to spot the biggest data hogs. You can hit pause on auto-updates or cut off background data for apps you don’t need running all the time. And for extra peace of mind, make sure your hotspot is secured with a strong password so only you are using your connection.
  • First things first — check your carrier's international roaming rates before you leave because they’re often incredibly expensive. Turn off data roaming in your phone settings and use Wi-Fi whenever possible at hotels, cafes, and airports.   • Limit high-data activities like streaming videos or downloading large files during work hours. Only set up a mobile hotspot when absolutely necessary and connect to available secure Wi-Fi networks whenever possible to preserve your mobile data. Also, choose productivity apps that are optimized for low data usage — email clients, document editors, and messaging apps typically consume minimal data compared to video conferencing or cloud storage syncing. Turn off automatic app updates and cloud backups while working on mobile data.
  • The easiest ways to slash your cellular data usage are:

    • Connect to Wi-Fi when available. Hotels, cafes, and airports offer free Wi-Fi access, but be cautious with public networks since they might be less secure.

    • Download content for offline use. Download Google Maps for your commute route, Spotify playlists for flights, and TV show episodes on Netflix for train rides while you're still on Wi-Fi.

    • Lower streaming quality. We suggest switching Netflix to standard definition and Spotify to normal quality — you'll barely notice the difference but save 60-70% of your data. It’s the same for other streaming apps, too.

    • Enable data saver modes. Most phones have built-in settings that compress images and limit background apps automatically.

    • Use Saily's security features. Saily’s web protection blocks malicious sites while the ad blocker reduces data usage by more than 28% by preventing bandwidth-heavy ads from loading.

    • Turn off automatic updates. Schedule app updates and photo backups for Wi-Fi-only times to avoid surprise data drains while traveling abroad.

Others charge overage fees that can get expensive quickly, especially with traditional carriers.   • When sharing data across multiple devices through a hotspot, use your eSIM provider's app to track total usage — your phone's built-in tracker only shows single-device consumption. Configure data-saving settings on each connected device by enabling data saver mode and turning off automatic updates to manage overall consumption across all your devices.
  • Yes, you can share your eSIM data with other devices using your phone's mobile hotspot feature. On iPhone, go to “Settings” > “Personal hotspot” and toggle it on. On Android, navigate to “Settings” > “Network and internet” > “Hotspot and tethering” > “Wi-Fi hotspot”.
    Keep in mind that sharing data through hotspot uses your plan's allowance faster since multiple devices are consuming data simultaneously!
  • Usually, your phone sticks to Wi-Fi when you’re connected, so your mobile data stays untouched. But watch out for features like Wi-Fi assist (on iPhones) or adaptive connectivity (on some Android devices). These can automatically switch you back to mobile data if your Wi-Fi signal gets shaky.
    Wi-Fi Assist on iPhones automatically uses cellular data when Wi-Fi connectivity is poor. Turn it off by going to “Settings” > “Cellular” > “Wi-Fi assist” and toggling it off. Adaptive connectivity on Android (also called “Smart connect” or “Intelligent Wi-Fi” on some devices) switches between Wi-Fi and mobile data for optimal performance. Disable it through “Settings” > “Network and internet” > “Wi-Fi” > “Wi-Fi preferences”.
    For frequent Wi-Fi signal drops, set up network priorities by "forgetting" unreliable networks in your Wi-Fi settings and only connecting to strong, stable connections. This prevents your phone from automatically connecting to weak signals that trigger the switch to mobile data.
  • Yes, your phone can still use data, even when you’re not using it. That’s because many apps run quietly in the background, syncing emails, refreshing feeds, sending notifications, or backing up your photos. If you want to turn background data off, head into your settings to limit or turn off background data for apps that don’t need to stay updated, or switch on your phone’s data-saver mode when you’re not on Wi-Fi.
  • For most activities, smartphones are actually designed to use less data than laptops. That’s because mobile apps often compress images and videos or load “lite” versions of websites to help save your data. Laptops, by contrast, tend to load the full versions of sites and larger files, which can quickly add up, even if you’re doing the same thing on both devices. However, if you’re streaming movies in HD or downloading big files, both devices can use a lot of data.
  • Data usage on YouTube, Netflix, and Disney+ tends to be the highest, especially if you stream videos in full HD. Social media favorites like Instagram, Snapchat, and TikTok also rack up plenty of data with auto-play videos and photos. Even navigation apps like Google Maps can use a surprising amount, especially when long trips necessitate using the app extensively or when you download offline maps. If you want to save data, try lowering video quality, turning off auto-play, or downloading your must-haves over Wi-Fi before you hit the road.
  • Downloading an app uses as much data as the app's file size. Therefore, the amount of data required to download an app varies greatly depending on the app itself. A simple mobile app, such as a calculator or a notepad, might only be a few megabytes (MB), sometimes even under 20 MB. However, more complex apps, such as mobile games, navigation tools, or video editing apps, can easily exceed 100 MB or 200 MB. In some cases, especially with large 3D games, the total install size can reach several gigabytes.
  • App updates use significant data, though usually less than a full download. The size depends on the update — small bug fixes might only need 5-50 MB, while updates with new features can hit 50-200 MB. Major updates for games or system apps can eat up hundreds of MB to gigabytes. We recommend checking these sizes before updating, especially when you're on mobile data. Save the big updates for when you're back on Wi-Fi to avoid burning through your data allowance on app improvements you might not even need.
  • Generally, email apps don’t use much data, especially if you’re just sending or receiving plain text messages. Sending one plain text email will use around 0.07 KB. It’s the emails with large attachments, like images or PDFs, that can start to use up more of your data — up to 5 MB per email. Another thing to keep in mind is how often your email app checks for new messages. Frequent syncing means more data usage, even if you’re not opening emails.
  • Messaging on mobile apps uses data in different amounts depending on what you're sending. Luckily, a lot of basic messaging uses very little data. Simple text messages (including emojis) use a very small amount of data, typically around 0.001 MB to 0.005 MB per message. Sending photos uses more data, generally between 0.1 MB and 2 MB per photo. The data usage depends on the photo's resolution and file size. Videos consume the most data, ranging from 500KB to 10MB or even more per video, depending on the length and quality. However, it's important to remember that data usage can vary significantly between different messaging apps. Apps like WhatsApp, Messenger, and Telegram often compress images and videos before sending them. This compression, while reducing data usage, also means the image or video you send isn't the same quality as the original file. Learn more on how much data iMessage uses and how much mobile data WhatsApp uses on our blog.
  • eSIMs don't use any data themselves — they're digital SIM cards that activate cellular plans without physical cards. Data consumption works exactly the same as traditional SIMs, depending entirely on what you’re doing — streaming, browsing, or using apps. Monitor your usage the same way through your phone's tracker or carrier app, since eSIM technology doesn't affect how much data your internet usage consumes.
  • No, using eSIMs doesn’t mean you’ll consume more data than with traditional roaming — data usage depends on your activities and network conditions, not whether your SIM is physical or digital. The advantage of eSIM is easier carrier switching, which can help you find better network coverage or more affordable data rates compared to expensive roaming charges.
